Naegele's Rule vs Ultrasound Dating: Which Due Date Method Is More Accurate?

A practical explainer on how the two most common pregnancy dating methods compare, and why your due date can shift after a scan.

Almost every pregnant person hears two different due dates at some point — one from a quick calculation based on their last period, and another, sometimes a few days off, from their first ultrasound. Neither number is wrong exactly, but they come from very different logic, and understanding that logic explains why obstetricians often trust one over the other depending on when in pregnancy you're being dated.

What Naegele's Rule Actually Does

Naegele's Rule is the traditional formula used to estimate a due date from the first day of the last menstrual period (LMP). The standard version adds one year, subtracts three months, and adds seven days to the LMP date — which is mathematically equivalent to simply adding 280 days, or 40 weeks, to that date. It's fast, requires no equipment, and has been used in obstetric practice since the 1800s.

The catch is that the formula assumes every person has a textbook 28-day cycle with ovulation occurring reliably on day 14. In reality, cycle length varies widely between individuals and even between cycles for the same person. Someone with a 35-day cycle typically ovulates later, so an LMP-based due date for them will usually run early — the pregnancy is often more advanced than the LMP math suggests. Irregular cycles, recent hormonal contraceptive use, and simple uncertainty about the exact first day of the last period all compound this problem.

What Ultrasound Dating Does Differently

Rather than working backward from a remembered date, first-trimester ultrasound dating measures the embryo or fetus directly — typically the crown-rump length (CRL) between roughly 6 and 13 weeks — and compares that measurement against standardized growth curves to estimate gestational age. Because early fetal growth is remarkably consistent across pregnancies during this window, CRL-based dating is considered the most accurate method available, generally accurate to within 3 to 5 days when performed before 14 weeks.

This is why, when an early scan disagrees with the LMP-based date by more than about 5 to 7 days, most obstetric guidelines recommend the scan date take precedence. It isn't that the ultrasound is inherently "better data" in some abstract sense — it's that it measures the pregnancy itself rather than relying on a proxy (last period date) that assumes a level of cycle regularity many people simply don't have.

Rule of thumb clinicians use: if a first-trimester scan and the LMP-based date agree within about a week, the LMP date is usually kept. If they disagree by more than that, the due date is typically redated to match the scan.

Why Ultrasound Gets Less Reliable Later

An important nuance: ultrasound is excellent for dating in the first trimester, but its dating accuracy declines the later in pregnancy it's performed. By the third trimester, fetal size is influenced by growth variation, genetics, and maternal health factors as much as by gestational age, so a third-trimester scan is a poor tool for establishing a due date — though it remains very useful for checking growth against an already-established date. This is why the "dating scan" ideally happens early, and later scans serve a different purpose: monitoring growth rather than confirming timing.

What This Means Practically

If you know your LMP with confidence and have regular, predictable cycles, Naegele's Rule and an early ultrasound will usually land within a few days of each other, and either can reasonably serve as your working due date. If your cycles are irregular, you conceived on a fertility medication or after stopping hormonal contraception, or you're simply unsure of your last period date, the ultrasound estimate is the one worth anchoring to.

It's also worth remembering that a due date, however it's calculated, is an estimate of the midpoint of a normal delivery window, not a deadline. Only a small minority of babies are actually born on their estimated due date; full-term delivery is considered anywhere from 37 to 42 weeks. The value of an accurate due date isn't precision for its own sake — it's that it anchors the timing of screening tests, growth assessments, and decisions about induction if a pregnancy goes past term.
